Risk Management and Position Sizing (Consistent Across Sources)

Ryan’s approach is asymmetric and mechanical: small, predefined risk per trade; never add to losers; concentrate in the best ideas only; and preserve capital aggressively in hostile markets.

- Per-position risk cap: Maximum 7% loss from entry on any single position. He cuts immediately at that level rather than hoping or averaging down. Most losses end up under 5–7% unless a gap occurs.43
- Initial sizing: Starts new positions at roughly 5% of capital. With a 7% stop, this creates ~0.35% portfolio risk per trade initially.
- Pyramiding: Adds only to winners that confirm (e.g., hold support on pullbacks to the 50-day or 21-day MA, or break to new highs while acting right). Never scales into losers.
- Portfolio concentration: Typically holds 4–5 strongest ideas once the picture clarifies. In the early stages of a new bull market he may start with 5–7 names and narrow down. He explicitly avoids spreading across dozens of positions.56
- Market context overlay: In choppy, volatile, or confirmed bearish conditions he goes heavily to cash or very small sizes. Recent IBD comments on high-ATR/vol stocks emphasize reducing position size further, concentrating even more, and selling into strength on quick moves rather than letting everything run.
- Overall philosophy: Capital preservation first. “The number one goal if you do anything is to preserve capital in a tough market.” He has stated losses are almost always under 10% (most under 5%) because entries are timed where upside should follow-through immediately if the thesis is correct.13

This framework produces the classic “few big winners, many small losers” payoff that compounded his championship results (161% in 1985, 160% in 1986, >100% in 1987 for a cumulative ~1,379% over three years in the U.S. Investing Championship stock division).

Entry Techniques

Ryan is primarily a breakout trader within the CAN SLIM framework but added pullback buying as a key adaptation.

- [[breakout]]: Buys stocks breaking out of proper bases (cup-with-handle, flat bases, etc.) on strong or increasing volume near the pivot point. Avoids chasing extended names far above the breakout. If missed, waits for a pullback into the buy zone rather than chasing.
- [[pullback]]: Buys dips to the 50-day or 21-day moving average [[key moving averages]] in an established uptrend, ideally on lighter volume (indicating selling pressure is drying up). Scales in over 2–3 days if support holds. Prefers stocks ~20% off highs for better risk/reward.
- Confirmation bias check: Strongly prefers stocks that show a profit on day one of the purchase. “Stocks should be at a profit the first day you buy them. In fact, having a profit on the first day is one of the best indicators that you are going to make money on the trade.” Non-confirming names are cut fast (<2 weeks).
- Filters: High [[relative strength]] (prefers 99), accelerating quarterly [[eps & sales]] (ideally 25%+), solid annual growth history, new products/catalysts, institutional sponsorship increasing, leadership in strong groups, and overall market uptrend. Screens hundreds to thousands of charts weekly. Avoids sub-$10 stocks.

He keeps a detailed trading journal noting exact reasons for every entry, add, and exit to study patterns and mistakes.

Exit Techniques — The Core of His Teachings

Ryan frames selling in three explicit components (repeated in IBD video sessions and materials):

1. Buying right and setting the initial stop (predefines risk mechanically).
2. Selling into strength.
3. Selling on weakness (breakdowns or loss of technical character).

He is “pretty much 100% technician” on exits even while using fundamentals for selection.55

[[sell into strength]] (profit taking / scaling out):  
Rule of thumb is to sell or trim when the stock reaches 20–25% above the buy point. MarketSmith/IBD materials explicitly highlight this zone (green box on charts) as the area for selling into strength. This is not always a full exit. He frequently takes partial profits, reduces exposure on the remaining position, and lets the strongest names continue if the “staircase” pattern (higher highs and higher lows) and technical picture remain intact.

On very rapid moves (e.g., 20% in a few days) he is more aggressive about taking some off. The goal is to compound by repeatedly locking gains and redeploying, while still participating in outliers. Conviction and ongoing technical strength matter—some names (e.g., certain IPO bases like OLLI in examples) are held well beyond 20–25% when acting right. He does not set rigid profit targets that force full exits; big winners can be held 6–12 months (or longer in powerful moves) as long as the technicals support it. Scaling out when overextended above moving averages is also mentioned as a way to protect gains while keeping some skin in the game.

Selling on weakness (protecting capital or gains):

- Hard stop: Full exit at the 7–8% level from entry (or relevant base low). No scaling out of losers—liquidate and move on. Stops are placed in advance and executed mechanically.
- Base re-entry rule (from Market Wizards): If a stock breaks out and then re-enters its prior base, sell at least 50% of the position. Often these names continue back to the lower end of the base, so quick partial (or full) exit is required. “Frequently, when a stock drops back into its base, it goes all the way back down to the lower end of the base. Therefore, you want to cut your losses quickly.” “Winning horses don’t back up into the gate.”
- Trendline / moving average breaks: Breaking the 50-day MA on above-average or heavy volume is a clear sell signal. Loss of the overall technical picture, relative strength deterioration, or signs of climactic/exhaustion behavior (volatility spikes, parabolic acceleration followed by failure) trigger exits to protect gains.
- Non-confirmation / fast failure: Anything that does not act right immediately is cut in days to under two weeks.

How Ryan stops “selling into weakness” (i.e., avoids emotional holding or panic selling at bad prices):  
By making exits mechanical and pre-defined before entry. The 7–8% stop and base re-entry rule remove discretion. He emphasizes reviewing every past trade (winners and losers) in a journal to identify patterns like “bought extended” or “held too long on breakdown” and drills the discipline to execute without rationalizing. In volatile or bearish periods he simply reduces exposure or goes cash rather than fighting. The philosophy is capital preservation first so powder is dry for the next proper setup. He has noted that emotional reactions (holding losers hoping for recovery or selling winners too early out of fear) are what destroy most traders; rules exist to override that.

Selling into strength vs. letting winners run:  
He does both via scaling. The 20–25% zone or overextension signals are used to take partial profits and de-risk. The strongest names with intact technicals and high conviction are allowed to [[let your winners run]] (months) because a small number of large winners drive the results. This is the explicit asymmetry: cut losers fast and mechanically; let the best ones compound while periodically harvesting strength to keep risk manageable.
