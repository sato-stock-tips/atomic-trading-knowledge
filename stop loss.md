
### stop loss placement conditions

stop loss placement should **preferably** try to be below:
1. [[key moving averages]]
2. [[bjorgum key levels]] and
3. a confluence of [[support & resistance]]
   wherever possible

condition 1: stop loss placements should not be more than 1x [[ATR%]] of the opening price and no less than 0.25x ATR% of the individual stock

condition 2: stop loss placements should try and be tight to avoid [[wide stops]] and allow for bigger [[position size]]

### stop losses enable larger positions and larger gains
a stop loss enables you to take much larger trades. risk management is done by either position size risk limit or risk of drawdown equity risk limit. 

### always place stops

A stop could be an actual stop placed on the brokerage, such as at the lows of the opening range breakout candle. A stop could also be a rules-based stop that has to be followed. Here are three reasons why stops are so important:

### stops protect capital

By keeping losses small. A small loss is a business expense. A big loss is a business ruined. [[small losses protect equity curve]]

### stops protect confidence

Every time you respect your stop, you reinforce discipline

### stops keep you in the game

Missing one winner hurts less than blowing up on one loser. Stops result in longer survival rate which gives more chances to hit a positive expected value payout

### respect Your stops

Never ignore your stops. Imagine if you were crossing the road and you always checked traffic both ways but never got hit once by oncoming traffic. Do you then stop to check both ways when crossing roads? Never! Stops are the checking of traffic equivalent.

### Deadstick landing

Deadstick landing is an aerospace term to explain landing a plane when its in the air with failed engines. A stock is most dangerous to your capital when you just opened it. As the stock proves itself and climbs to higher heights, there can be more leeway. Its very similar to taking off in a plane. The moment of takeoff is the most dangerous as any engine failure would give the pilot very little time and space to react, as compared to when the plane is high in the skies. Same goes for trading.

That’s why your stop should be closest the moment of trade opening.

Cutting Losses Is Like Buying an Insurance Policy

This policy of limiting losses is similar to paying small insurance premiums. You're reducing your risk to precisely the level you're comfortable with. Yes, the stock you sell will often turn right around and go back up. And yes, this can be frustrating. But when this happens, don't ever conclude you were wrong to sell it. That exceedingly dangerous thinking will eventually get you into serious trouble.
Think about it this way: If you bought insurance on your car last year and you didn't have an accident, was your money wasted? Will you buy the same insurance this year? Of course you will! Did you take out fire insurance on your home or your business? If your home or business hasn't burned down, are you upset because you feel you made a bad financial decision? No. You don't buy fire insurance because you know your house is going to burn down. You buy insurance just in case, to protect yourself against the remote possibility of a serious loss.
It's exactly the same for the winning investor who cuts all losses quickly. It's the only way to protect against the possible or probable chance of a much larger loss from which it may not be possible to recover.
If you hesitate and allow a loss to increase to 20%, you will need a 25% gain just to break even. Wait longer until the stock is down 25%, and you'll have to make 33% to get even. Wait still longer until the loss is 33%, and you'll have to make 50% to get back to the starting gate. The longer you wait, the more the math works against you, so don't vacillate. Move immediately to cut out possible bad decisions. Develop the strict discipline to act and to always follow your selling rules.
Some people have gone so far as to let losing stocks damage their health. In this situation, it's best to sell and stop worrying. I know a stockbroker who in 1961 bought Brunswick at $60 on the way down in price. It had been the market's super leader since 1957, increasing more than 20 times. When it dropped to $50, he bought more, and when it dropped to $40, he added again. When it dropped to $30, he dropped dead on the golf course.
