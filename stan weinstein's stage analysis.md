_Weinstein four stages — 30-week MA_

**Stage 1 — the base.** Sideways, ignored, range-bound after a decline. The 30-week average flattens; volume is dead; the public is bored or bitter. Smart money quietly accumulates from discouraged sellers.

**Stage 2 — the advance.** Price breaks out above the base on expanding volume and rides above a clearly _rising_ 30-week average, making higher highs, outperforming the market. Volume swells on the up-weeks and dries up on [[pullback]]. This is the part you want to own.

**Stage 3 — the top.** The advance stalls. The 30-week average flattens and begins to roll over. Price action turns erratic and choppy — wider swings, failed breakouts, no net progress — and the volume signature _inverts_: now the down-weeks carry heavier volume than the up-weeks. That is the fingerprint of distribution — institutions selling into a still-euphoric public, the early money handing stock to the late money, while the headlines stay bullish.

**Stage 4 — the decline.** Price below a falling 30-week average, lower highs and lower lows, relative strength collapsing.

Every stock you will ever own is somewhere on the same journey.

It builds a base. It breaks out and advances. It tops and churns. It declines. Then — if it survives — it builds a base again, and the cycle repeats.

Stan Weinstein gave this cycle a name and a map: Stage Analysis. Four stages, one moving average, and a simple discipline that has guided traders for nearly four decades. It’s one of the most elegant frameworks in all of technical analysis — and once you see it, you can’t un-see it. Every chart you look at, you’ll start asking: which stage is this?

Where CAN SLIM tells you which stock deserves your attention, Weinstein tells you where in its lifecycle that stock currently sits — and therefore whether to buy, hold, or run.

The Origin: A Crash, and a Better Way
Stan Weinstein developed Stage Analysis after living through a brutal market crash in 1962, while still in college. Like most investors then, he relied on fundamental analysis — and watched many of his investments lose serious value in the downturn.

Instead of giving up, he turned to technical analysis and studied thousands of charts. Over time, a consistent pattern emerged: stocks formed bases, broke out into uptrends, lost momentum, and eventually declined — then repeated the cycle. Inspired by Edwards and Magee’s Technical Analysis of Stock Trends, he refined these observations into a four-stage system.

He shared it through his newsletter, The Professional Tape Reader, and then in his 1988 classic, Secrets for Profiting in Bull and Bear Markets — still one of the best technical analysis books ever written. Decades later, the framework holds up remarkably well, because it’s built on something that doesn’t change: human behaviour in markets.

📘 The book: Secrets for Profiting in Bull and Bear Markets by Stan Weinstein (1988)

If you read only one technical analysis book in your life, make it this one. It’s clear, practical, and — unlike most trading books — it has aged almost perfectly. Everything in this deep dive comes from it. Roughly 350 pages, no fluff, and the four-stage framework alone is worth ten times the cover price.

The One Tool You Need: The 30-Week Moving Average
Before the stages, understand the single most important line on a Weinstein chart: the 30-week moving average (roughly the 150-day MA).

This line is your trend referee. Its slope and the stock’s position relative to it define everything:

Stock below a falling 30-week MA → trouble.

Stock whipsawing around a flattening 30-week MA → basing.

Stock above a rising 30-week MA → strength.

Almost every stage call comes back to this one relationship. Keep it in mind as we walk through the four stages.




Four Stages, Trader Lion
Stage 1 — The Base
What it is: The recovery ward. Stage 1 happens after a stock has finished a long decline (Stage 4). The downtrend has stopped, and the stock begins moving sideways as supply and demand reach equilibrium.

How to spot it:

Sideways price action. The stock oscillates in a range between support and resistance. This can last weeks, months, or even years.

A flattening 30-week moving average. This is the critical signal. While the stock was crashing, the MA sloped sharply down. Now it flattens out — the worst is likely over. The stock whipsaws above and below it without strong momentum either way.

Balance between buyers and sellers. Neither side is in control, which is exactly why the price goes sideways.

Declining volume early, then rising. Volume drops off as the aggressive Stage 4 selling exhausts itself. But as the base matures, volume starts to pick up — especially on up days. That returning volume is the tell that buyers are coming back and a Stage 2 breakout may be near.

What to do: Watch, don’t buy. A stock can sit in Stage 1 far longer than your patience would like. The temptation is to buy “before the breakout” to get a better price. Resist it. You want confirmation, not a head start that turns into dead money for two years.

Stage 2 — The Advance
What it is: The money stage. This is where the strong uptrend lives and where the vast majority of gains are made. The stock breaks out of its Stage 1 base and begins a sustained climb.

How to spot it:

A breakout on high volume. The stock breaks through resistance on 2–3× its average daily volume. That volume surge is institutions stepping in — and their demand is what fuels the move. A breakout without volume is suspect.

Price above rising moving averages. The stock trades above its 30-week (and 200-day) moving averages, and crucially, those averages are now sloping upward. The most powerful moves also hold above a rising 50-day MA.

Higher highs and higher lows. The signature of a healthy uptrend. Each pullback finds support at a higher level than the last.

Respecting the 50-day MA. Pullbacks are normal, but the stock should stay above a rising 50-day average. A sharp close below it is a warning the trend may be weakening.

The ideal buy point: The Stage 2 breakout itself — the moment the stock clears resistance on heavy volume. This is the highest-probability entry in the entire system.

The alternate buy point — the pullback / retest: Sometimes after breaking out, a stock pulls back to retest its old resistance level (now acting as support). If that retest happens on lighter volume and holds at or just above the breakout point, it’s a second-chance entry — often lower-risk than the initial breakout, because you’ve now seen the level hold. This is the buy point for those who missed the initial move or want tighter risk.

This maps directly to Weinstein’s own distinction between the “Ideal Buy for Investor” (the clean breakout from a long base, near the 30-week MA) and the “Ideal Buy for Trader” (the continuation breakout from a tight consolidation higher up in an established uptrend). Same framework, two entry styles, depending on whether you’re early or adding to strength.

Stage 3 — The Top
What it is: The distribution phase. Stage 2 can’t last forever. Eventually demand fades, supply increases, and the uptrend loses its strength. The stock stops advancing and starts churning sideways — but this sideways looks very different from Stage 1.

How to spot it:

Slicing below the 10-week (50-day) MA on heavy volume. One of the first red flags that the uptrend is breaking down.

Erratic, volatile price action. Wild swings up and down. These aren’t signs of strength — they’re instability. The upward spikes are often late buyers rushing in just as long-term holders quietly sell. That’s the “churn.”

Churning on heavy volume. The stock moves sideways but with heavy volume on both up and down days — a sign sellers are distributing stock into the hands of latecomers without the price making real progress.

Failed breakout attempts. The stock tries to make new highs, but the breakouts come on weak volume and fail quickly, falling back into the range.

Flattening 30-week and 200-day MAs. The long-term averages that were rising in Stage 2 now flatten and roll over. The stock whips above and below them.

What to do: Reduce or exit. Stage 3 is where you protect the gains you made in Stage 2. The distinction between Stage 1 (basing) and Stage 3 (topping) is subtle but vital: same sideways shape, opposite meaning. The clue is what came before (an advance, not a decline) and the heavy two-sided volume.

Stage 4 — The Decline
What it is: The mirror image of Stage 2 — a sustained downtrend. The stock breaks below the support it built in Stage 3, and the slide begins.

How to spot it:

A break below Stage 3 support. This confirms the downtrend. Sell immediately when this happens, even if the moving averages are still flat.

Lower highs and lower lows. The signature of a downtrend, the inverse of Stage 2.

Trading below falling long-term MAs. The 30-week and 200-day averages slope downward, and price stays beneath them.

Weak rallies on light volume. Bounces happen, but they fail quickly. Each failed rally is a short-selling opportunity for aggressive traders.

Declines don’t need heavy volume. Unlike Stage 2 breakouts, stocks can “fall under their own weight” — a simple lack of buyers is enough. Heavy selling volume just signals stronger downside.

What to do: Be out. Weinstein’s advice is to “take the oath” — never hold a stock in Stage 4. It often begins while the news is still good, trapping investors who believe in the fundamentals. Don’t try to catch the bottom; stocks fall further than anyone expects. Wait for a genuine Stage 1 base before even thinking about buying again.

The Whole Cycle, in One Breath
A stock declines hard (Stage 4), exhausts its sellers, and goes sideways as the 30-week MA flattens (Stage 1). Volume returns, it breaks out on heavy buying above rising averages (Stage 2 — buy here), advances in higher highs and higher lows, then loses momentum and churns on heavy two-sided volume as the averages flatten (Stage 3 — sell here), and finally breaks support into a new decline (Stage 4 — be gone). Then it bases again. Forever.

How to Find the Ideal Buy Point
Putting it together, the highest-probability buy is the Stage 2 breakout — but not all breakouts are equal. Weinstein’s filters:

Volume confirmation. Demand at least 2× average volume, ideally 3×. That surge is institutional money confirming the move. No volume, no trust.

Sector strength. Stocks rarely move alone — they follow their sector. A breakout in a leading sector has far better odds than the same setup in a lagging one. Check whether the stock’s industry group is leading or lagging the broad market before you commit.

The two entry styles again:

Investor’s buy: the clean breakout from a long Stage 1 base, close to the 30-week MA — maximum upside, requires patience for the base to complete.





Trader’s buy: the continuation breakout from a tight flag or consolidation, higher up in an established Stage 2 uptrend, above a rising 30-week MA — lower base, but you’re buying confirmed strength.





Both are valid. Which you use depends on your temperament and timeframe.


My Take: Why a Long-Term Investor Studies Stage Analysis
I’ll say the same thing I said about CAN SLIM this week: I’m not primarily a trader. At arvy I’m a long-term quality investor.

So why does Stage Analysis matter to me?

Because it’s the cleanest mental model for trend that exists, and trend awareness helps every investor — even buy-and-hold ones. Knowing the difference between a Stage 1 base and a Stage 4 decline is the difference between buying a stock that’s bottoming and “catching a falling knife” that has years left to fall. The “take the oath — never hold Stage 4” discipline has saved more capital than any clever entry ever made.

Stage Analysis and CAN SLIM pair beautifully. CAN SLIM tells you which stocks have the fundamental and institutional firepower to be big winners. Stage Analysis tells you when in their lifecycle to act — and, just as importantly, when to step aside. One is the engine, the other is the map.

Where I’d add the honest caveat: like all technical timing systems, Stage Analysis demands discipline and consistent screen time. The signals only work if you act on them — every time, without the “this one’s different” exceptions we all love to make. For most people juggling a career and a family, a disciplined long-term approach will outperform a half-applied stage system. But even if you never trade a single breakout, learning to read the four stages will make you a calmer, sharper investor. You’ll know where you are on the map.

Three Sentences to Remember
Every stock cycles through four stages — base, advance, top, decline — and the 30-week moving average is your map.

The ideal buy is the Stage 2 breakout on 2–3× volume; the alternate is the lower-risk pullback that retests and holds the breakout level.

Take the oath: never hold a stock in Stage 4, no matter how good the story sounds.
